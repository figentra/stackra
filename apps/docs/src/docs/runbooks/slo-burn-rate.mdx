<Note>
  Applies to a product that declares objectives in its root `runway.yaml`
  (`observability.slos.objectives`, STA-2854). Objectives start at the first paying tenant (ADR 0184
  decision 3); before then no burn alert exists.
</Note>

An objective promises a share of good requests over a window (30 days by default): availability, the requests
answered without a server error, or latency, the requests answered within a threshold. The **error budget** is the
rest: at 99.9% over 30 days, 0.1% of the requests may fail, about 43 minutes of full outage. A **burn rate** is how
fast the budget goes: a burn rate of 1 spends it in exactly the window, 14.4 spends it in about two days.

Every objective gets four alerts, the multi-window, multi-burn-rate set of the Google SRE workbook. Each fires only
while the error ratio is above its threshold over a long window and over a short one a twelfth as long, so it fires
fast and stops soon after the burn stops.

| Severity           | Burn rate (30 days) | Long / short window | Budget spent at that rate | Goes to                     |
| ------------------ | ------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| `critical` (page)  | 14.4                | 1h / 5m             | 2% in an hour             | `notify.critical` (on call) |
| `critical` (page)  | 6                   | 6h / 30m            | 5% in six hours           | `notify.critical` (on call) |
| `warning` (ticket) | 3                   | 1d / 2h             | 10% in a day              | `notify.warning`            |
| `warning` (ticket) | 1                   | 3d / 6h             | 10% in three days         | `notify.warning`            |

With another `slos.windowDays` the burn rates scale so each row still spends the same share of the budget.

## Read the alert

The alert is `SloErrorBudgetBurn`. Its labels say what burns:

- `slo_service`: the service member (`orders`).
- `slo`: `availability` (5xx answers) or `latency` (answers slower than the objective's threshold).
- `severity`, `long_window`, `short_window`: which row of the table fired.

The summary gives the burn rate and the description the share of the budget that row spends.

## Respond

<Steps>
  <Step title="Acknowledge and check it still burns">
    Acknowledge the page. In Grafana (or Prometheus), graph `slo:sli_error:ratio_rate5m{slo_service="<service>",slo="<indicator>"}`
    over the last hours. Still above the threshold: the burn is live, go on. Back under it: the burn stopped; the
    alert clears within the short window, and you go to the last step.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find what changed">
    Compare the start of the burn with the deploy log (`stackra runway status`, the release train), a feature flag
    change, a dependency incident (database, cache, a provider's status page) and a traffic jump. For availability,
    group `http_server_request_duration_seconds_count` by `http_route` and `http_response_status_code` to find the
    failing route; for latency, compare the threshold bucket per route. Traces of the slow or failing route
    (`service.name` = the service) show the span that fails or waits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Stop the burn first">
    Roll back the release that started it (`stackra runway rollback`), turn the flag off, or shed the load. Fix
    forward only when a rollback is not possible. A page means the budget is gone in hours: mitigate before you
    diagnose further.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ticket: plan the fix">
    A `warning` alone is a slow burn: no page, but the budget runs out within days. Open (or take) the ticket, find the
    cause in working hours, and fix it before the budget is spent.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Record what the budget lost">
    Note in the incident or ticket the start, the end, the burn rate and the share of the budget spent. When a burn
    spends more than a fifth of the window's budget, write a post-incident review (ADR 0185). With the budget spent,
    releases of that service wait for reliability work until the window recovers.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## When the alert is wrong

- **Fires with no traffic problem**: the service label may not match. The rules read `job` (Prometheus's OTLP
  receiver writes `service.name` there) and `http_server_request_duration_seconds`; a backend that names them
  differently passes `metric` and `serviceLabel` to `prometheusSloRules`.
- **Never fires for latency**: the threshold must be a bucket bound of the histogram (5, 10, 25, 50, 75, 100, 250, 500,
  750, 1000, 2500, 5000, 7500 or 10000 ms); the plan refuses any other value, and a backend with other bounds needs a
  matching threshold.
- **Too noisy**: the objective is stricter than the service can hold. Change the target in `runway.yaml` in a reviewed
  change; never silence the alert.

## How the rules are made

`observabilityPlan` (`@stackra/infra-observability`) reads `observability.slos` and derives the four alerts of each
objective; `prometheusSloRules(plan)` renders them as one Prometheus rule group per objective (recording rules
`slo:sli_error:ratio_rate<window>` and the alerting rules), loaded by the LGTM stack's ruler (ADR 0184 decision 2).
`promtool test rules` proves in the package's integration tests that a fast burn pages, a slow burn opens a ticket and a
healthy service stays quiet.

```yaml
observability:
  slos:
    windowDays: 30 # default
    objectives:
      - service: orders
        availability: 99.9 # percent of requests without a 5xx
        latency: { thresholdMs: 500, percent: 99 } # percent of requests within 500 ms
```
